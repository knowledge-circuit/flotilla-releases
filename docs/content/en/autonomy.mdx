You make the decisions an agent should not make alone. You do not want to manage the agents. Autonomy is the part of Flotilla that keeps those two apart.

It is one setting per project with four levels. It is off by default, and each level includes the one before.

| Level | What it adds |
|---|---|
| Off | Nothing. Flotilla behaves as described on the other pages. |
| See | Each thread shows what is true of it and who has to act next. |
| Decide | An inbox of what is yours. Agents ask you there, with options. |
| Act | Agents follow pull requests and issues that are not theirs, and draft what they would post in your name. |

## The problem it solves

Without autonomy, four things go wrong as a fleet grows.

- **Facts are told once and then forgotten.** If the turn that heard about a failing CI run went wrong, nothing shows the failure again.
- **Only one thread sees them.** An orchestrator hears about its own worker's CI through a relay, two model calls later.
- **A finished turn looks the same whether it is a report or a question.** So you read everything.
- **Nobody watches an idle orchestrator.** One that sits with open tasks and no worker running stalls without a sound.

## The idea: who acts next

Blocked, ready and stale are all answers to one question: who has to act next on this thing?

<Flow>{`flowchart LR
  facts[Facts from git, GitHub and threads] --> cond[Conditions]
  cond --> owner{Owner}
  owner -->|worker or orchestrator| agent[Shown to the orchestrator every turn]
  owner -->|you| inbox[Your inbox]
  owner -->|outside| wait[Nothing sent, the clock runs]`}</Flow>

**Facts** are read from the things themselves, such as a thread's state, a plan's open tasks, a pull request's CI and its review threads.

**Conditions** are worked out from the facts every time they are read, and never stored. A condition is something true of a thread that somebody has to act on, such as "CI failing" or "idle with open tasks". It holds for as long as the facts say so, whoever was told.

**Owners** are one of four: a worker, an orchestrator, you, or the outside. The outside is anything no agent and not you can move, such as CI that is still running.

| Condition | Owner |
|---|---|
| A worker's pull request has conflicts, failing CI, unresolved review threads or changes requested | Worker |
| A worker failed | Orchestrator |
| A worker is idle with no pull request for longer than "Stale after" | Orchestrator |
| A pull request is merged and its worker is not settled | Orchestrator |
| The same, but settling would lose something | You |
| An orchestrator is idle with open tasks and no worker working | Orchestrator. This is the stall. |
| CI is running, or a required review is pending | Outside |
| A pull request is green with no conflicts and no open review threads | You, to merge |
| An orchestrator failed | You |

<Callout>
Every open thing in a project has exactly one owner at any time, and Flotilla knows how long that owner has been idle. Nothing waits on nobody.
</Callout>

A condition is stale when its owner has not moved for longer than "Stale after". A stale condition shows in red on its thread and gets a card in your inbox. That is the whole escalation. There is no count and no repeat.

## See

A sidebar row gets a second line while a condition holds. It shows the condition, a small icon for its owner and how long it has held. The tooltip lists every condition on the thread.

Each orchestrator also gets its own conditions and its workers' at the start of every turn. An orchestrator sees its worker's failing CI on its next turn with no relay.

Pull request facts refresh every 60 seconds while a page shows the project.

## Decide

An "Inbox" row appears at the top of the thread list, with a count.

An orchestrator that needs your choice asks with a decision card. The card has a title, a short context and two to four options, and the agent marks the one it would take. The agent does the thinking. You read four lines.

You answer with a click, with the keys `1` to `4`, or with a line of your own words. The answer goes to the asking thread as a message and starts a turn. Asking does not block the agent, which goes on with everything that does not depend on the answer.

The inbox also holds cards the server makes itself, for a pull request that is ready to merge, a failed orchestrator and anything stale. A ready pull request links to GitHub. No card merges.

Threads stay the way to go deep. You can still open any thread and answer there, and the agent then closes the card.

At this level `needs you` changes meaning. The amber dot means the thread has an item in your inbox. A reply you have not opened is a quieter mark, a faint ring around the dot.

Workers cannot ask you directly. They reach you through their orchestrator.

## Act

You also work with people. You review their pull requests and answer their issues. At this level you paste a link into an orchestrator and ask it to follow the item.

The link becomes a watched task in the plan. When your review is requested, or the author answers or pushes after your last review, the orchestrator reads the change and drafts a review or a comment.

Other people read those words as yours. So the draft comes to your inbox first, as a card with three fixed options: post it, post it with my edit, drop it. On "post", the orchestrator posts with `gh` or the Linear MCP. The server posts nothing itself.

The inbox header counts how many drafts you posted unchanged, edited or dropped. That share is the measure of whether approval per post could be loosened later.

Flotilla reads the facts of a watched pull request only when it is in one of the project's own repos. For a pull request elsewhere, or a Linear issue, the orchestrator reads the item itself when it wakes.
